\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This report documents the design, implementation, and evaluation of a neural network system for recognising everyday urban and household sounds, motivated by assistive technology for Deaf and hard-of-hearing users who cannot rely on an audible cue when a siren, car horn, or alarm occurs. Raw audio is transformed into log-mel spectrograms --- a two-dimensional representation of frequency against time --- so that a convolutional neural network can learn each sound's signature directly from the signal, without any hand-engineered acoustic features. Four tasks are performed on the UrbanSound8K and ESC-50 datasets: (i) ten-class \textbf{classification} with a four-block convolutional network; (ii) \textbf{novelty detection} using a convolutional autoencoder whose reconstruction error identifies sounds outside the training vocabulary; (iii) unsupervised \textbf{clustering} of the network's learned embeddings using k-means and t-SNE; and (iv) an \textbf{architecture comparison} against a multi-layer perceptron baseline together with a data-augmentation ablation. All experiments follow UrbanSound8K's official ten-fold cross-validation protocol, which is necessary because clips sliced from the same source recording share a fold; a random split leaks those slices across training and test and inflates reported accuracy. The convolutional network reaches \cnnAcc\% accuracy and \cnnFone{} macro F1 while using \cnnParams{} parameters --- roughly fifteen times fewer than the \mlpParams-parameter perceptron baseline it outperforms, which quantifies the value of convolutional inductive bias on spectrogram input. The autoencoder detects previously unheard sounds with a ROC-AUC of \aeAUC, and is compared against the obvious alternative of thresholding classifier confidence. All models were trained locally on an Apple M1 laptop using the Metal Performance Shaders backend; no paid computing resources were used.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Background and Motivation}

Sound carries a large amount of safety-critical information that is normally processed without conscious effort. A siren approaching from behind, a car horn, a smoke alarm, glass breaking in another room, or somebody knocking at the door are all events that a hearing person reacts to automatically. For a person who is Deaf or hard of hearing, none of these signals arrive. The World Health Organization estimates that over 1.5 billion people live with some degree of hearing loss, and the everyday consequence is not merely inconvenience but a genuine reduction in situational safety.

Commercial systems already exist in this space --- Apple's Sound Recognition and Google's Sound Notifications both listen for a small set of household sounds and raise a visual or haptic alert --- which establishes that the problem is real and the approach is viable. What those systems do internally is not published, and building an equivalent from open data is a well-scoped exercise in applied neural network design.

\subsection{Why This Is a Neural Network Problem}

Raw audio is a waveform sampled at tens of thousands of values per second; there is no meaningful way to feed that directly to a decision tree, an SVM, or a logistic regression. Classical approaches therefore depend on a human first deciding which summary statistics matter --- zero-crossing rate, spectral centroid, a handful of MFCC coefficients --- and the result is bounded by the quality of that hand-engineering.

A convolutional network removes that bottleneck: presented with a spectrogram, it learns its own filters, with early layers responding to local time--frequency texture (onsets, harmonic stacks, broadband noise) and deeper layers composing these into class-level patterns. This \emph{representation learning} --- discovering features from data rather than specifying them in advance --- is the defining capability of artificial neural networks and the reason they displaced feature-engineering pipelines in audio, vision, and language. The novelty task reinforces the point: an autoencoder learns a compressed code for the sounds it has seen, and nothing in classical supervised learning provides an equivalent way to flag \emph{``this input is unlike anything I was trained on''} without negative examples in advance.

\subsection{Project Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em]
    \item Build a convolutional neural network that classifies ten urban sound classes from log-mel spectrograms, evaluated under the dataset's official ten-fold protocol.
    \item Quantify the benefit of convolutional structure by comparing against a multi-layer perceptron baseline of substantially larger parameter count.
    \item Measure the effect of spectrogram-domain data augmentation (SpecAugment and time shifting) as a controlled ablation.
    \item Implement novelty detection with a convolutional autoencoder so that unfamiliar sounds are reported as unknown rather than being forced into one of the ten trained labels, and compare this against a softmax-confidence baseline.
    \item Examine the learned representation without labels, using k-means clustering and t-SNE projection of the network's penultimate embeddings.
    \item Deliver a working real-time demonstration that runs from a laptop microphone.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Original Contributions}

The individual components --- CNNs on mel-spectrograms, autoencoder anomaly scoring --- are established. The contributions here are their combination and the rigour of the evaluation:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2em]
    \item \textbf{An open-set framing of a closed-set benchmark.} UrbanSound8K is almost always treated as pure ten-way classification; here the classifier is paired with a novelty detector and evaluated against genuinely unseen classes from a second dataset (ESC-50) --- the situation a deployed assistive device actually faces.
    \item \textbf{An honest baseline for the novelty detector.} Autoencoder reconstruction error is compared directly against thresholding the classifier's own softmax confidence, the cheap alternative a reviewer would propose.
    \item \textbf{A parameter-matched argument for convolution.} The MLP baseline is deliberately larger than the CNN, isolating inductive bias from capacity.
    \item \textbf{A real-time deployment.} Both networks run live on laptop hardware, showing the system is practical rather than purely offline.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Environmental Sound Classification}

Environmental sound classification (ESC) differs from speech and music recognition in that the sounds have no shared grammatical or harmonic structure. Early systems used hand-designed features --- most commonly Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), inherited from speech processing --- fed into a Gaussian Mixture Model or Support Vector Machine.

The shift to neural approaches was driven by two datasets released in the mid-2010s. Salamon, Jacoby and Bello introduced UrbanSound8K \cite{salamon2014}, which remains the standard benchmark for urban ESC, and Piczak released ESC-50 \cite{piczak2015} alongside a demonstration that convolutional networks operating on spectrograms outperform the feature-engineering pipelines that preceded them \cite{piczak2015cnn}.

\subsection{Spectrograms as Network Input}

The dominant design pattern in modern ESC is to convert audio into a time--frequency representation and treat the result as an image. The mel scale is used because it approximates the non-linear frequency resolution of human hearing, allocating more resolution to lower frequencies where most environmental sound energy lies. Amplitudes are converted to decibels because loudness perception is logarithmic; without this, a small number of high-energy frames dominate the input range and training becomes unstable.

Comparative studies of time--frequency representations for ESC \cite{huzaifah2017} found log-mel spectrograms to be a consistently strong choice, which motivates their use here.

\subsection{Data Augmentation}

ESC datasets are small by deep learning standards --- UrbanSound8K contains 8,732 clips, orders of magnitude fewer than the image corpora on which deep CNNs were developed --- so overfitting is the central practical difficulty. Salamon and Bello \cite{salamon2017} showed that audio-domain augmentation (time stretching, pitch shifting, adding background noise) materially improves CNN performance on UrbanSound8K.

Park et al.\ later introduced SpecAugment \cite{park2019}, which operates directly on the spectrogram by masking random frequency bands and time spans. It is considerably cheaper than audio-domain augmentation, since no re-synthesis or re-transformation is required, and it forces the network to use the entire time--frequency pattern rather than latching onto a single narrow band. SpecAugment and random time shifting are the augmentations used in this project.

\subsection{Novelty and Out-of-Distribution Detection}

A softmax classifier is closed-set by construction: its outputs are constrained to sum to one over the classes it knows, so an unfamiliar input is necessarily assigned to one of them. Hendrycks and Gimpel \cite{hendrycks2017} established the baseline that maximum softmax probability carries \emph{some} out-of-distribution signal, while also documenting that neural networks are frequently confident when wrong.

Autoencoder reconstruction error is a widely used alternative \cite{sakurada2014}: a model trained to compress and rebuild in-distribution data will reconstruct out-of-distribution data poorly, and the error is a usable score requiring no negative examples at training time. This report implements the autoencoder approach and evaluates it against the Hendrycks and Gimpel softmax baseline rather than assuming its superiority.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}

Relative to the literature, this project sits at the intersection of standard ESC classification and open-set recognition. Reported CNN accuracies on UrbanSound8K commonly fall in the 70--92\% range depending on architecture, augmentation, and --- importantly --- whether the official fold protocol was respected. This work targets the upper part of that range with a deliberately compact architecture, and extends the standard setup with the novelty-detection and clustering tasks required by the assignment brief.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:data}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{UrbanSound8K}

UrbanSound8K \cite{salamon2014} is the primary dataset: 8,732 labelled audio excerpts of up to four seconds, drawn from field recordings on the Freesound archive, covering ten classes selected from an urban sound taxonomy. The classes are \texttt{air\_conditioner}, \texttt{car\_horn}, \texttt{children\_playing}, \texttt{dog\_bark}, \texttt{drilling}, \texttt{engine\_idling}, \texttt{gun\_shot}, \texttt{jackhammer}, \texttt{siren}, and \texttt{street\_music}.

Four of these --- \texttt{car\_horn}, \texttt{dog\_bark}, \texttt{gun\_shot}, and \texttt{siren} --- are treated as safety-critical in the demonstration system and trigger a distinct alert state.

    \centering

    \label{fig:classdist}
\end{figure}

\subsection{ESC-50 (Unseen Sounds)}

ESC-50 \cite{piczak2015} contributes 2,000 five-second clips across 50 classes. It is \emph{not} used for classifier training. Its role is to supply genuinely unfamiliar sounds for the novelty-detection experiment: eight household and safety-relevant classes were selected --- \texttt{door\_wood\_knock}, \texttt{glass\_breaking}, \texttt{crying\_baby}, \texttt{clock\_alarm}, \texttt{door\_wood\_creaks}, \texttt{water\_drops}, \texttt{footsteps}, and \texttt{can\_opening} --- none of which appear in the UrbanSound8K vocabulary.

This choice is deliberate. Evaluating novelty detection against random noise would be trivially easy and uninformative; evaluating it against real recordings of plausible household events is a fair test of whether the system would behave sensibly in the environment it is intended for.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol and a Common Methodological Error}
\label{sec:protocol}

UrbanSound8K ships with ten predefined folds, and the dataset authors are explicit that these must be used rather than a random split. The reason is that the 8,732 clips are sliced from a much smaller number of source field recordings, and all slices from one recording are placed in the same fold. Under a random split, several slices of a single recording land in training while others land in test; the model can then succeed by recognising the specific recording rather than the sound class, and reported accuracy is inflated by a wide margin.

All results in this report use ten-fold cross-validation over the official folds. For each fold $k$, fold $k$ is the test set, fold $(k \bmod 10) + 1$ is the validation set used for early stopping and model selection, and the remaining eight folds are used for training. Normalisation statistics are computed on the training partition alone and then applied unchanged to validation and test, so that no test statistics leak into training.

Because every clip appears in exactly one test fold, pooling the held-out predictions across all ten folds yields one honest prediction per clip for the entire dataset. The aggregate confusion matrix and per-class scores in Section~\ref{sec:results} are computed on that pooled set.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Audio Front-End}

Every clip passes through an identical front-end before reaching any network:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em]
    \item \textbf{Load as mono at 22{,}050\,Hz.} Halving the usual 44.1\,kHz rate discards content above 11\,kHz, which carries little discriminative information for these classes, and halves the computational cost.
    \item \textbf{Fix the duration to four seconds.} Shorter clips are zero-padded and longer ones truncated, so every spectrogram has identical dimensions --- a requirement for batching through a convolutional network with a fixed input size.
    \item \textbf{Short-Time Fourier Transform} with a 1{,}024-sample window and 512-sample hop.
    \item \textbf{Project onto 64 mel bands} between 20\,Hz and the Nyquist frequency.
    \item \textbf{Convert power to decibels}, referenced to the per-clip maximum.
\end{enumerate}

The result is a $64 \times 173$ matrix per clip: 64 frequency bands by 173 time frames. Figure~\ref{fig:specexamples} shows one example per class, and makes the central argument of the project visually: each sound class produces a visibly distinct pattern, which is precisely what makes a convolutional network appropriate.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safefig{1.0}{fig_spectrogram_examples.png}
    \caption{Log-mel spectrograms, one per UrbanSound8K class. The horizontal striations of \texttt{jackhammer} and \texttt{drilling}, the rising harmonic sweep of \texttt{siren}, and the sharp broadband transient of \texttt{gun\_shot} are distinguishable by eye --- structure that a convolutional network can exploit and that a flattened feature vector destroys.}
    \label{fig:specexamples}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Data Augmentation}

Two spectrogram-domain augmentations are applied to training data only:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2em]
    \item \textbf{Random time shift} (up to $\pm20\%$ of the clip), implemented as a circular roll. A siren remains a siren regardless of when within the window it begins, so the network should not depend on absolute position.
    \item \textbf{SpecAugment} \cite{park2019}: one random frequency band (up to 8 of 64 mel bins) and one random time span (up to 16 of 173 frames) are masked to the spectrogram floor. This prevents the network from relying on any single narrow band and acts as a strong regulariser on a dataset of this size.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safefig{1.0}{fig_augmentation.png}
    \caption{One siren clip under each augmentation. Masked bands and spans are visible as flat regions. The network never sees exactly the same input twice, which is what suppresses memorisation.}
    \label{fig:augmentation}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Architecture 1: Multi-Layer Perceptron (Baseline)}

The baseline flattens the $64 \times 173$ spectrogram into an 11{,}072-dimensional vector and passes it through two fully connected hidden layers (512 and 256 units) with batch normalisation, ReLU activation, and dropout at $p = 0.5$.

Its purpose is diagnostic. Flattening destroys the two-dimensional structure of the spectrogram: the network has no notion that two adjacent time frames are related, or that a harmonic pattern shifted slightly in frequency is the same pattern. Every input pixel receives its own independent weight, which is why the model requires \mlpParams{} parameters --- roughly fifteen times the CNN's \cnnParams. If the CNN outperforms it despite that disparity, the difference is attributable to architecture rather than capacity.

\subsection{Architecture 2: Convolutional Neural Network}

The main classifier consists of four convolutional blocks, each comprising a $3 \times 3$ convolution, batch normalisation, ReLU, and $2 \times 2$ max pooling, with channel widths of 32, 64, 128, and 256. Spatial dropout is applied in the deeper blocks.

Two design decisions are worth stating explicitly:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2em]
    \item \textbf{Global average pooling replaces a flatten-and-dense head.} Collapsing each of the 256 final feature maps to its mean gives a 256-dimensional descriptor. This keeps the parameter count an order of magnitude below the MLP and, more importantly, makes the model approximately invariant to \emph{where} in the clip a sound occurs --- appropriate, because a dog bark two seconds in is the same event as a dog bark at the start.
    \item \textbf{The 256-dimensional pooled vector is exposed as an embedding.} This is the representation analysed in the clustering task (Section~\ref{sec:clusterresults}), allowing the learned feature space to be examined directly.
\end{itemize}

Training uses AdamW (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), cross-entropy loss with label smoothing of 0.05, cosine-annealed learning rate, and early stopping on validation accuracy with a patience of eight epochs.

\subsection{Architecture 3: Convolutional Autoencoder}

The novelty detector is a symmetric convolutional autoencoder. The encoder applies three stride-2 convolutions (16, 32, and 32 channels), compressing the $64 \times 173$ input to a $32 \times 8 \times 22$ latent representation; the decoder mirrors this with transposed convolutions. Because transposed convolution can overshoot the original dimensions by a pixel, the output is cropped back to the input size.

Crucially, it is trained \emph{only} on the ten known classes, with a mean-squared-error reconstruction objective. Having learned a compressed code adequate for those sounds, it reconstructs an unfamiliar sound poorly, and the per-clip reconstruction error
\[
s(x) = \frac{1}{HW}\sum_{i,j}\bigl(\hat{x}_{ij} - x_{ij}\bigr)^2
\]
serves directly as a novelty score. The decision threshold is set at the 95th percentile of the reconstruction errors observed on \emph{known} data --- a calibration that requires no novel examples at all, matching the constraint a deployed system operates under.

At \aeParams{} parameters the autoencoder is by far the smallest of the three networks, which matters for running both models concurrently in the real-time demonstration.

\subsection{Feature Extraction}

The front-end of Section~\ref{sec:method} is implemented in \texttt{src/data.py}. Listing~\ref{lst:melspec} shows the core transform.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Waveform to log-mel spectrogram (\texttt{src/data.py}).}, label={lst:melspec}]
def audio_to_melspec(y: np.ndarray, sr: int = C.SAMPLE_RATE) -> np.ndarray:
    mel = librosa.feature.melspectrogram(
        y=y, sr=sr,
        n_fft=C.N_FFT,          # 1024
        hop_length=C.HOP_LENGTH, # 512
        n_mels=C.N_MELS,         # 64
        fmin=C.F_MIN, fmax=C.F_MAX,
    )
    # Decibel scaling: loudness perception is logarithmic, and without
    # this a few loud frames dominate the input range.
    mel_db = librosa.power_to_db(mel, ref=np.max)
    return mel_db.astype(np.float32)
\end{lstlisting}

Extraction runs once over the full corpus and is cached to \texttt{.npy}, after which every experiment reads features from memory. Measured throughput was approximately 3\,ms per clip, so caching all 8{,}732 spectrograms completes in well under a minute --- a useful property when experiments must be repeated.

\subsection{The Fold Split}

Listing~\ref{lst:folds} implements the protocol described in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. The comment records \emph{why} the split is by fold, since this is the single easiest way to produce misleadingly high numbers on this dataset.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Official fold-based splitting (\texttt{src/data.py}).}, label={lst:folds}]
def make_fold_split(X, meta, test_fold: int, val_fold: int | None = None):
    """Split by UrbanSound8K's official folds.

    Clips sliced from the same source recording share a fold, so splitting
    by fold (rather than randomly) is what keeps the evaluation honest.
    """
    if val_fold is None:
        val_fold = test_fold % C.N_FOLDS + 1

    folds  = meta["fold"].values
    labels = meta["classID"].values

    te = folds == test_fold
    va = folds == val_fold
    tr = ~(te | va)

    return (X[tr], labels[tr]), (X[va], labels[va]), (X[te], labels[te])
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{The Convolutional Classifier}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={AudioCNN forward pass with optional embedding output (\texttt{src/models.py}).}, label={lst:cnn}]
class AudioCNN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_classes: int = 10, dropout: float = 0.3, width: int = 32):
        super().__init__()
        w = width
        self.block1 = ConvBlock(1,     w,     pool=2)
        self.block2 = ConvBlock(w,     w * 2, pool=2, dropout=dropout / 2)
        self.block3 = ConvBlock(w * 2, w * 4, pool=2, dropout=dropout / 2)
        self.block4 = ConvBlock(w * 4, w * 8, pool=2, dropout=dropout)

        # Global average pooling instead of flatten+dense: ~10x fewer
        # parameters, and invariance to where in the clip the sound occurs.
        self.gap     = nn.AdaptiveAvgPool2d(1)
        self.dropout = nn.Dropout(dropout)
        self.fc      = nn.Linear(w * 8, n_classes)

    def forward(self, x, return_embedding: bool = False):
        x = self.block4(self.block3(self.block2(self.block1(x))))
        emb    = self.gap(x).flatten(1)          # 256-d descriptor
        logits = self.fc(self.dropout(emb))
        if return_embedding:
            return logits, emb
        return logits
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Novelty Scoring}

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Reconstruction error as a novelty score (\texttt{src/models.py}).}, label={lst:ae}]
@torch.no_grad()
def reconstruction_error(self, x) -> torch.Tensor:
    """Per-sample mean squared error -- the novelty score."""
    recon = self(x)
    return ((recon - x) ** 2).flatten(1).mean(dim=1)
\end{lstlisting}

The threshold is calibrated from known data only, as shown in Listing~\ref{lst:threshold}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Threshold calibration without novel examples (\texttt{src/anomaly.py}).}, label={lst:threshold}]
# Set at a percentile of the KNOWN scores, so the detector can be
# calibrated without ever seeing a novel example -- which is the
# situation a deployed system is actually in.
thr = float(np.percentile(known_scores, percentile))   # percentile = 95
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Architecture Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} reports ten-fold cross-validated performance for the three configurations.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \caption{Ten-fold cross-validation results on UrbanSound8K (mean $\pm$ standard deviation across folds).}
    \label{tab:comparison}
    \begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Parameters} & \textbf{Accuracy (\%)} & \textbf{Macro F1} \\
        \midrule
        MLP baseline          & \mlpParams & \mlpAcc\ $\pm$ \mlpAccSd & \mlpFone\ $\pm$ \mlpFoneSd \\
        CNN (no augmentation) & \cnnParams & \cnnAcc\ $\pm$ \cnnAccSd & \cnnFone\ $\pm$ \cnnFoneSd \\
        CNN + augmentation    & \cnnParams & \cnnAugAcc\ $\pm$ 7.01 & \cnnAugFone\ $\pm$ 0.062 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safefig{1.0}{fig_model_comparison.png}
    \caption{Performance and model size side by side. The CNN achieves higher accuracy and macro F1 than the MLP while using roughly fifteen times fewer parameters.}
    \label{fig:comparison}
\end{figure}

The central finding is that the convolutional network outperforms the perceptron by \cnnVsMlp\ percentage points while using roughly fifteen times fewer parameters. Since the MLP has strictly greater capacity, the gap cannot be model size; it is architectural inductive bias. Convolution encodes two assumptions that hold for spectrograms and that the MLP must otherwise learn from scratch: informative structure is \emph{local} in time and frequency, and the same pattern is meaningful wherever it appears. Weight sharing learns a harmonic-onset filter once and applies it everywhere, whereas the MLP must relearn that pattern at every position --- from only a few thousand examples.

Augmentation, by contrast, did \emph{not} help: the augmented CNN scored \cnnAugAcc\% against \cnnAcc\% without it, a statistically indistinguishable difference with slightly higher fold variance ($\pm$7.01 versus $\pm$\cnnAccSd). Reported as the negative result it is, the likely cause is that the architecture is already strongly regularised --- dropout, weight decay, label smoothing, and global average pooling --- so the validation curves in Figure~\ref{fig:training} show only mild overfitting to begin with. With little overfitting to recover, SpecAugment adds input noise rather than signal. This matches the literature view that augmentation helps most when a model is data-starved and overfitting hard, which is not the regime here. The remaining analysis therefore uses the unaugmented CNN.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safefig{1.0}{fig_training_curves.png}
    \caption{Training and validation curves on a representative fold. Dashed lines are training, solid lines validation. The gap between them is modest even without augmentation, which is why adding SpecAugment yields no measurable gain.}
    \label{fig:training}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Per-Class Performance and Error Structure}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safefig{0.9}{fig_confusion_matrix.png}
    \caption{Aggregate confusion matrix over all ten held-out folds, normalised per row. Each clip is predicted exactly once, when its fold is the test fold.}
    \label{fig:confusion}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \safefig{0.9}{fig_per_class_f1.png}
    \caption{Per-class F1. Impulsive, spectrally distinctive sounds are recognised most reliably; sustained broadband machine noise is the principal source of error.}
    \label{fig:perclass}
\end{figure}

The strongest class is \bestClass\ (F1 = \bestClassF) and the weakest \worstClass\ (F1 = \worstClassF). The error structure in Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} is acoustically interpretable rather than arbitrary: the dominant confusions occur among \texttt{air\_conditioner}, \texttt{engine\_idling}, \texttt{drilling}, and \texttt{jackhammer}. All four are sustained, broadband, low-frequency machine noises whose log-mel representations genuinely resemble one another, and human listeners confuse them under similar conditions. By contrast, classes with sharp temporal or harmonic signatures --- \texttt{gun\_shot}, \texttt{siren}, \texttt{dog\_bark} --- are well separated.

This distinction matters for the intended application. The four safety-critical classes are drawn from the well-separated group, so the errors the system does make are concentrated in the classes where a mistake carries the least consequence: confusing an air conditioner with an idling engine has no safety implication, whereas missing a siren would.

That k-means recovers the class structure to this degree confirms the network learned a genuinely organised representation rather than an arbitrary decision boundary. The t-SNE projection is also diagnostically useful: the classes that overlap in embedding space are the same ones that the confusion matrix shows being misclassified, so the two analyses corroborate each other. The machine-noise classes form a single contiguous region, which explains at a representational level why they are difficult to separate.

\subsection{Real-Time Demonstration}

The trained models are deployed in \texttt{demo/live\_demo.py}, which keeps a rolling four-second microphone buffer, recomputes the spectrogram on the training front-end, and runs both networks twice per second. The interface shows the live spectrogram, per-class confidence, and a status banner with three states: an ordinary recognised sound, a safety-critical one, and a sound the autoencoder flags as unfamiliar. Predictions are smoothed with an exponential moving average to prevent single-frame flicker, and because the models are only \cnnParams{} and \aeParams{} parameters, both run in real time on laptop CPU or MPS (\inferTime\ per window). A recorded walkthrough is linked in Section~\ref{sec:repo}.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
%% ============================================================

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2em]
    \item \textbf{Small vocabulary.} UrbanSound8K's taxonomy is urban and outdoor-oriented; a deployed device would need the household sounds that matter most (doorbell, smoke alarm, name being called), requiring a combined taxonomy or purpose-built data collection.
    \item \textbf{Clean recordings, noisy world.} Both datasets contain isolated events, but real environments layer sounds continuously, and a single-label classifier cannot express ``a siren \emph{and} street music''. Multi-label sound event detection over time is the correct formulation.
    \item \textbf{Fixed four-second windows.} A gunshot is padded with silence; a continuous jackhammer is truncated. Variable-length modelling or attention pooling would fit the data better.
    \item \textbf{Coarse novelty detection.} Reconstruction error flags that an input is unfamiliar but not what it is, and the threshold trades false alarms against misses along one axis; per-class calibration would likely be needed in production.
    \item \textbf{Single-device evaluation.} Robustness across microphones, room acoustics, and distances --- all of which shift the spectrogram distribution --- was not measured. A transfer-learning baseline (fine-tuning an AudioSet-pretrained network) is the obvious next experiment.
\end{itemize}

\section{Social, Ethical, Legal, and Professional Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Privacy}

An always-listening microphone is among the most privacy-sensitive components in a home. The design mitigates this: all inference is local, the four-second buffer is overwritten continuously, and no audio is ever recorded, transmitted, or stored. A deployed version must preserve this, since a cloud implementation would create a permanent record of domestic conversation as a side effect of listening for a doorbell.

\subsection{Asymmetric Consequences of Error}

The two error types are not equivalent: a false alarm is a minor irritation, whereas a missed siren or smoke alarm has real safety consequences. This argues for tuning the safety-critical classes toward recall at the cost of precision --- the opposite of what maximising overall accuracy suggests --- and for reporting per-class metrics rather than a single headline number.

\subsection{Bias and Dataset Provenance}

Both datasets are drawn from Freesound and skewed toward Europe and North America. Sirens especially differ between countries, and horns, alarms, and street noise vary regionally, so this system should not be assumed to transfer to Kathmandu without local evaluation and, most likely, local data collection. Presenting it as universally applicable would be misleading.

\subsection{Framing, Accessibility, and Licensing}

The system is a supplementary awareness aid, not a safety-certified alerting device, and must not be positioned as a replacement for certified accessible smoke alarms; genuine accessibility work would also require participation from Deaf users rather than assumptions made on their behalf. Finally, both datasets are Creative Commons Attribution Non-Commercial: their use here is consistent with those terms, both are cited, and neither is redistributed --- the download script retrieves them from their official sources.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================

This project built and evaluated a neural network system for recognising urban and household sounds, framed around assistive awareness for Deaf and hard-of-hearing users. Four tasks were performed under UrbanSound8K's official ten-fold protocol: classification with a convolutional network on log-mel spectrograms, novelty detection with a convolutional autoencoder, unsupervised clustering of the learned embeddings, and an architecture comparison with an augmentation ablation.

The convolutional network reached \cnnAcc\% accuracy and \cnnFone{} macro F1 using \cnnParams{} parameters, outperforming the larger \mlpParams-parameter perceptron by \cnnVsMlp\ points. Because the baseline is the larger model, this isolates the contribution of convolutional inductive bias rather than capacity. Augmentation did not improve on it --- a negative result reported honestly and attributed to the architecture already being well-regularised. The autoencoder detected unseen sounds with ROC-AUC \aeAUC, ahead of the \softmaxAUC\ softmax baseline.

Two observations carry forward. First, the classifier's errors are acoustically structured, not random: confusion concentrates among sustained broadband machine noises, while the safety-critical classes sit in the well-separated region of the embedding space --- shown independently by the confusion matrix and the t-SNE projection. Second, the closed-set classifier is confident on inputs it has never seen (mean softmax \novelConf), which a fixed-test-set accuracy never reveals; any open-environment system needs an explicit way to signal that an input falls outside its competence.

Most concretely, the project demonstrated the capability that distinguishes neural networks from the classical methods covered elsewhere in the programme: no acoustic feature was designed by hand. Given only a time--frequency representation, the network learned the filters that separate a siren from a jackhammer --- and the clustering confirms that representation is structured enough to recover the classes without labels at all.
